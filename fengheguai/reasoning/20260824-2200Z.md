**Hypothesis (pre-registered).** Gradient clipping appears in ZERO of 504 nodes. Having declared the campaign saturated I went looking for mechanisms that are standard everywhere else and simply absent here; clipping, label smoothing and dropout are the three. Label smoothing biases predictions away from the true distribution, which is precisely what bpb measures, and dropout at roughly one epoch over a large corpus is usually harmful -- both have poor priors. Clipping does not.

The specific reason it might matter here: clipping globally would be near a no-op for the matrices, because Muon orthogonalises its updates and their step size is set by the algorithm rather than by the gradient norm. But the AdamW groups take raw Adam steps at unusually large learning rates -- embeddings at 0.6, value embeddings at 0.3, per-layer scalars at 0.5 -- and that is where a single outlier batch can move a weight further than the schedule intends. So this clips the AdamW groups only, which is both the motivated subset and the one where the effect is not structurally cancelled.

The model also carries a `loss > 100` fast-fail, which suggests spikes were a live concern at some point in its history. That is circumstantial rather than evidence, and I am not treating it as more.

Safety of the group filter is established by existing code -- the training loop already does `if group['kind'] == 'muon'` for every group unconditionally, so the key is present on all of them. The edit asserts no clipping is already present and that the optimizer-step block matches. Dry-run verified against t0500.

**Predicted effect.** Throughput cost should be small but not zero: clip_grad_norm_ reads every AdamW gradient to compute a norm, which is the embeddings and lm_head -- about 62M parameters, ~250MB per step. That is roughly 0.05ms against a 105ms step, so I expect steps flat to -0.5%.
On the mechanism I expect a null. Adam is already scale-invariant in its update magnitude, which absorbs most of what clipping would do, and 2880 steps of a well-tuned schedule is not a regime where single-batch outliers usually dominate. Range -0.001 to +0.001.
The case where it wins is if the campaign's large embedding learning rates -- which were tuned alongside every other constant and may be sitting at the edge of stability -- occasionally overshoot. If it does win, the follow-up is not a tighter clip but asking whether those learning rates are too high, since clipping would then be masking a schedule problem.

A subtler risk: clipping interacts with the weight-decay schedules, which ramp across the run. If a clip fires late when decay is near zero, the effective update composition differs from early. I cannot separate that from the main effect in one trial.
